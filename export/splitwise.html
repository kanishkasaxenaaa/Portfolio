The Question

Why do people keep using WhatsApp to settle what Splitwise already tracks?

Splitwise has the data. It knows who owes what. But users still screenshot the app, paste it into a chat, and pay separately — because the app never closes the loop. This redesign explores what happens when you fix that.

01
Problem

The app optimizes tracking.
Users need closure.

Users aren't frustrated because Splitwise is hard to use. They're frustrated because the moment they need it most — settling up — it falls apart. The gap isn't UX. It's product.

What Splitwise does well

  • ✓ Tracks group expenses
  • ✓ Calculates debt simplification
  • ✓ Shows who owes whom
  • ✓ Manages multiple groups

Where users hit walls

  • ✗ "Settle Up" sends you out of the app
  • ✗ Non-users can't participate at all
  • ✗ Groups buried alphabetically, not by recency
  • ✗ Free plan limits block basic daily use

Root Cause

Splitwise overemphasizes expense logging while neglecting settlement clarity, inclusive participation, and navigation that matches how people actually use groups.

The 5 Whys analysis traced every major user pain point back to one decision: the product was designed for tracking, not for resolution.

"Splitwise should make my life easier, not harder. I need it to manage all my group expenses in one place without pushing me to pay for basics."
— Pragya, 28, Marketing Associate · Survey Participant
"I just want the app to handle the math, include friends who don't use it, and give me a dashboard I can trust without having to pay for every little thing."
— Vasu, 27, Engineer · 1:1 Interview

Key Behavioral Patterns

01

Settlement is the most painful moment — and it's broken

Users feel settlement is incomplete because money still moves outside the app. They want a seamless pay-and-close loop, not a redirect to another app.

02

Non-users are a dealbreaker, not an edge case

Most friend groups include at least one person without the app — a landlord, a parent, a new acquaintance. Forcing app registration kills the whole group's workflow.

03

Group discovery is backwards

Groups sorted A–Z means the most active trip or flatmate group is buried. Users scan top-to-bottom, not alphabetically — the nav fights their mental model.

04

The free plan erodes trust, not just utility

A 3-entry daily cap doesn't just inconvenience — it signals "we don't trust you." Users compare to fully-free competitors and feel cheated, not upsold.

05

Users want insight, not just numbers

84% of surveyed users wanted visual dashboards and analytics. They're not asking for spreadsheets — they want to understand their patterns at a glance.

03
Product Direction

What this redesign intentionally avoided

The competitive landscape is full of apps that added features without fixing the core. Tricount is free but rough. YNAB is thorough but not social. The opportunity isn't to build more — it's to close the loop Splitwise already opened.

Avoided

More tracking features

Splitwise already tracks well. Adding receipt scanning or budget categories wasn't the gap — completing the settlement flow was.

Avoided

Gamification overlays

Streaks and rewards improve engagement metrics but not trust. Financial tools need reliability, not cheerleading.

Avoided

Aggressive upselling

Mid-task paywalls are the single biggest trust destroyer. The redesign moves Pro features to genuine power-user territory, not basic function walls.

Focused On

One-tap UPI settlement

Pay directly from the app. Auto-log the transaction. Mark as settled. No app-switching, no manual notes.

Focused On

Guest & placeholder mode

Non-users get a link via SMS or WhatsApp. They see their share, pay via UPI, and the transaction syncs back — no account required.

Focused On

Activity-first navigation

Groups sorted by recency and activity, not alphabet. Pin the ones that matter. Surface pending balances instantly on open.

Usability Testing · Iteration

What broke in testing

Two rounds of usability evaluation — heuristic review and task-based testing with 3 participants using the Think-Aloud method — surfaced friction before it became permanent. Each observation directly shaped an iteration.

Observed Friction → Design Response

Before
Terms like "Commitment", "Limit", and "App Lock" confused users. They didn't know what committing meant in a financial context.
→
After
Renamed to plain-language equivalents: "Spending Goal", "Category Cap", "Focus Mode". Each action includes a one-line description of what it does.
Before
Users couldn't find the chatbot / AI guidance trigger. It blended into the interface with no clear entry point.
→
After
AI guidance surfaced as a persistent "Quick Actions" card on the dashboard with a distinct visual treatment and clear label.
Before
Transaction list was dense and hard to scan — users said it "felt like a spreadsheet" and avoided it.
→
After
Grouped by date with category icons, color-coded debt direction (owed/owing), and collapsible sections for older activity.
Before
Users confused rewards with savings features — the reward system felt unrelated to financial management.
→
After
"Top Splitters" leaderboard repositioned as a social/fun layer, clearly separated from financial data. Optional to show.
Before
Desktop sidebar was long and cluttered — users had to hunt for their most-used groups among alphabetically sorted lists.
→
After
Sidebar replaced with activity-sorted group list. Pinned groups appear at top. Recent activity timestamp replaces group name as primary visual hierarchy.

Heuristic Evaluation

5

critical issues

Most violations were in Recognition over recall (unclear icons, unlabeled actions) and Error prevention (no undo for destructive actions like marking settled).

Each was addressed in the second iteration. The third pass focused purely on visual hierarchy and reading flow.

07
Reflection

What actually shifted

This project started as a dashboard redesign. It ended as a product strategy exercise. The hardest decisions weren't visual — they were about what not to build.

01 · Product Thinking

The problem was never the UI

Every usability complaint traced back to a product decision: a paywall, a missing integration, an assumption that everyone in a group has the app. Redesigning the interface without addressing these would have been cosmetic.

02 · Research Translation

Survey data doesn't design solutions — it points at them

84% of users wanting "visual dashboards" doesn't mean build more charts. It means users feel out of control and want clarity. The design response was simpler navigation and a clear net-balance state — not more data.

03 · Iteration Discipline

The best testing insight was about terminology, not layout

Users understood the intent of features but failed on language. "Commitment" meant nothing. "Spending Goal" worked immediately. This shifted how I write interface copy — label the action, not the concept.

04 · Future Direction

What I'd test next

Cross-group settlement consolidation (same person in 3 groups → one payment), notification behavior during active trips, and whether the Guest Mode conversion rate justifies the engineering investment. Also: does the dark mode actually build more trust, or is that a hypothesis?
