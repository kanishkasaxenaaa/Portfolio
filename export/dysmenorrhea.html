The Real Tension

Not the pain.
The silence around it.

Dysmenorrhea affects up to 95% of menstruators globally — and 80% go untreated. Not because solutions don't exist, but because the culture of silence, inaccessible infrastructure, and tracking-first apps leave people to push through alone.

01
Apps track, don't support. 64% of period apps log symptoms. Only 10% include any pain relief. Data without action.
02
54% push through silently. Social stigma and fear of judgment prevent people from seeking help — even when pain scores 8/10.
03
Disability compounds everything. The wheelchair user said it most clearly: she has never found a single space that meets both needs simultaneously.

What research revealed

Behavioral insights from
the margins

Four interviews, 78 surveys, and one contextual inquiry with a user navigating campus on a wheelchair during her period. These are the patterns that shaped every design decision.

01 — Concealment
Nobody discloses the real reason
All 4 interviewees hid their pain. Claiming "headache" or "stomach bug" felt safer than saying period. Stigma operates through silence.
02 — Infrastructure
Home is the only fallback
Every respondent eventually leaves the space. No campus, office, or public environment has ever adequately served them. The exit is the coping mechanism.
03 — Communication
Verbal interaction is a barrier
For Tanya (deaf & mute), asking for help is impossible. For others, explaining "why" is humiliating. Any support system must work without words.
04 — Policy gap
Policy alone doesn't protect
Mansi has 11 paid menstrual leave days and medical documentation. She still fears her manager's response. Culture must change alongside design.
05 — Sensory
Bright lights and noise worsen pain
3 of 4 interviewees cited sensory overload as a pain amplifier. Dim lighting isn't a preference — it's accessibility.
06 — Disability
Accessibility + menstrual needs never coexist
The wheelchair user had never found a single space that met both needs simultaneously. It's not a gap — it's a complete absence.
"The problem is not menstrual pain — it is the absence of discreet, real-time, and inclusive support systems in environments that expect uninterrupted productivity."

Usability testing & iteration

What changed after real users

Testing with 4 participants across disability profiles surfaced real friction — and forced better decisions.

Friction observed
"I couldn't find the pain mode when I needed it — I was looking for a button but it was in a menu."
→ Long-press on the heart icon, always visible in nav
Removed all navigation from the critical path. In pain, you need one action.
Friction observed
"The onboarding asked too many questions before I could just try it."
→ Progressive disclosure: 3 essential questions, rest deferred
Clinical profile builds over time. Trust must come before data collection.
Design refinement
Privacy masking on pain mode
Users reported anxiety about colleagues seeing the app open. Pain mode now overlays a neutral "system update" screen. Toggle controlled by user.
Accessibility refinement
High-contrast + large touch targets in pain mode
During pain, fine motor control degrades. Minimum 48px touch targets, increased contrast, reduced visual noise in all pain-state screens.

What changed in thinking

Designing from
the extremes
toward the center

This project started as a health app. It became something different — a study in how public systems fail bodies that don't conform to the "uninterrupted productivity" ideal.

The wheelchair user who has never found a single space meeting both her disability and menstrual needs — designing for her made every decision clearer and better for everyone.

Biggest shift
I started designing for average pain. Research forced me to design for 8/10 pain — which meant rethinking every interaction for impaired cognition, limited motor control, and emotional distress. Every improvement for extreme users improved the base experience.
Tradeoff made
Chose privacy and discretion over feature visibility. The most powerful features (SOS, clinical passport) are hidden behind low-friction paths — not highlighted. Trust before utility.
What I'd do next
Prototype the physical Pause Kit dispenser UX. The digital and physical systems must feel like one product — and right now only the app exists. The hardware touchpoint needs the same design care.
Future scope
Institutional integration — workplace HR portals, campus systems, and manager-facing dashboards that reduce the burden on the individual to advocate for themselves.
