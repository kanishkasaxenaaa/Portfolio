The Tension

Not the pain.
The silence around it.

People experiencing severe menstrual pain are still expected to function — attend class, work, commute, perform — even when movement itself becomes difficult. Public spaces offer no recovery. Systems offer no support. And culture demands silence.

01 — Push Through Culture
54% keep going despite severe pain
Not because they want to. Because no support system exists and explaining pain feels worse than enduring it quietly.
02 — Infrastructure Void
Every public space fails them
Campus, office, transit, washroom — none are designed for pain recovery. Home becomes the only safe option. The exit is the coping mechanism.
03 — Disability × Menstruation
The intersection no one designs for
Wheelchair users, deaf & mute users, and those with sensory impairments carry a triple burden — disability, gender, and menstrual taboo — simultaneously.

Research synthesis · Pause · 2026
Research → Design

Four insights.
Four decisions.

Every feature in Pause connects directly to a user behavior observed in research. These are the four that shaped the product's core.

01 — Concealment
"I'd rather say I have a headache than explain period pain."
↓ design response
Discreet interactions that require zero explanation
Silent SOS, privacy-masked screens, pre-written messages. No verbal disclosure ever needed.
02 — Sensory Pain
"Bright lights and noise make everything 10x worse."
↓ design response
Sensory-safe mode with low stimulation UI
Pain mode dims the entire interface. Reduced animation, muted tones, calm pacing. Sensory design as accessibility.
03 — Communication Barrier
"She cannot ask for help. The app has to do it for her."
↓ design response
Icon-first, non-verbal interaction throughout
Designed for Tanya (deaf & mute). One tap sends a pre-written, dignified message to a trusted contact silently.
04 — Tracking ≠ Support
"Apps log my pain. None of them help during it."
↓ design response
Real-time intervention, not data collection
Pause skips the logging-first paradigm. The moment pain hits, the product activates. Action before analytics.
Product Direction

What it isn't. What it is.

✗ Not this
×
A period tracker
64% of apps track. 10% help. Pause doesn't track first.
×
A wellness app with pink aesthetics
Generic femtech tropes. Calm ≠ cute. This is serious design.
×
Onboarding-heavy, data-hungry
In pain, multi-step flows are unusable. Trust before data.
×
Designed for able-bodied users only
15% of respondents had a disability. Exclusion is a design failure.
×
Requiring verbal or social disclosure
Stigma makes asking for help harder than the pain itself.
✓ This
✓
A real-time support companion
Activates when pain peaks. Action-first interface.
✓
Calm, minimal, clinically grounded
Editorial tone. No infantilizing design. Respect by default.
✓
One long-press to activate everything
Zero navigation during distress. Muscle memory over menus.
✓
Inclusive by architecture, not afterthought
Every UI decision tested against motor, sensory, and communication constraints.
✓
Silent dignity as a design principle
Support that never demands explanation. Privacy masks, pre-written messages, non-verbal flows.

Feature Showcase

Every feature
earned its place

Nothing added for novelty. Each decision traces back to a research observation, an accessibility need, or a moment of documented distress.

🌑
Sensory Safe Mode
Bright lights and noise physically amplified pain for 3 of 4 interviewees. The interface needed to become part of the recovery, not an obstacle to it.
Dim UI Reduced motion Muted tones Sensory accessibility
👆
One-Hand Navigation
Pain + mobility constraints reduced interaction ability. Temporary disability (post-accident) and permanent disability both require bottom navigation and large touch zones optimised for thumb reach.
Bottom nav 48px min targets Thumb zone
🤫
Silent SOS
Designed specifically for Tanya (deaf & mute). But also for everyone who has ever felt too embarrassed to explain their pain. Dignity first — the app asks so you don't have to.
Non-verbal Pre-written Privacy masked
📍
Pause Kit Locator
Every respondent eventually went home because no public space offered recovery. The Pause Kit network makes campuses and workplaces survivable — physical support via digital discovery.
Map-based QR collection No staff interaction
📋
Clinical Passport (GA34)
Culture must change alongside policy. The Clinical Passport gives users verifiable documentation of dysmenorrhea severity — shifting the burden of proof from the person in pain to a certified medical record.
ICD-11 GA34 Telehealth Verified badge
🔮
7-Day Prep Routine
Reactive panic vs. proactive preparation. The week before predicted pain peak, Pause activates a personalized micro-routine — hydration, sleep, nutrition, rest scheduling. Preparation as intervention.

Inclusive Design

Accessibility is the
product, not a feature

Every UI decision was evaluated against four disability profiles — temporary, permanent, sensory, and communication. Not at the end. From the first sketch.

⚡
Temporary Disability from Pain
Pain itself is a temporary disability. It reduces motor control, cognitive capacity, sensory tolerance, and communication ability. Pause was designed for the worst moment — pain at 8/10 — which means every other moment is easier too.
♿
Mobility Impairment
Wheelchair users explicitly stated washrooms, vending, and rest spaces are never co-designed for both disability and menstruation. Pause Kit locations are flagged for wheelchair access, lowered fixtures, and caregiver space.
🔇
Communication Barriers
Designed for Tanya — deaf & mute. Icon-first interactions throughout. Silent SOS. Pre-written messages. AAC compatibility. No feature requires verbal communication to function.
👁️
Sensory Sensitivity
Pain mode is dim, slow, and quiet. Reduced contrast in bright elements. Animations reduced to near zero. Low visual noise. Designed for migraines and light sensitivity as a direct accessibility response.
01
Reduced Steps
Every critical action reachable in 1–2 taps. During distress, every extra step is a barrier.
02
Icon-First UI
Primary navigation uses symbols and icons. Text is secondary. Communication-agnostic by design.
03
Privacy First
Caregiver access never compromises user dignity. Privacy masking hides the app from view when needed.
04
Dual-Access Spaces
Pause Kit locations flagged for both wheelchair access and menstrual support — the first space to hold both needs.
Speculative Impact

What Pause
is designed to do

🌿
Reduce silent suffering
By lowering the barrier to seeking help, Pause could shift 54% of "push through" users toward earlier, gentler support-seeking — before pain peaks become crises.
🏛️
Make public spaces survivable
Designed to help campuses and workplaces become recovery-aware environments — not through policy alone, but through infrastructure that acknowledges pain exists.
🛡️
Shift the burden of proof
The Clinical Passport moves documentation from the user's personal plea to a certified medical record — aiming to reduce the emotional labor of justifying pain to institutions.
♿
Normalize intersectional care
By co-designing for disability + menstruation, Pause challenges the assumption that these are separate concerns — modelling what true inclusive health infrastructure looks like.
🧠
Reduce cognitive load in crisis
One-tap activation, no menus, pre-written messages, and predictive preparation collectively aim to reduce decision fatigue at the exact moment users can afford it least.
💬
Start institutional conversations
The tool itself is a provocation. Every Pause Kit installed in a campus washroom is a statement: invisible pain is real, and our environments should reflect that.
Reflection

Designing beyond
the interface

This project started as a health app. It became something harder — a study in how public systems fail bodies that don't conform to the "uninterrupted productivity" standard.

The wheelchair user who has never found a space meeting both her disability and menstrual needs simultaneously taught me what designing from the margins actually means. Not as a metaphor. As a literal brief.

Pain is invisible. So is good inclusive design — until someone who needed it finally feels held by a system instead of failed by one.

View interactive prototype →
Biggest shift
I began designing for average pain. Research forced me to design for 8/10 pain — impaired cognition, limited motor control, emotional distress. Every improvement for the extreme case improved everything else.
Hardest tradeoff
Privacy vs. feature visibility. The most powerful features (SOS, Clinical Passport) are behind low-friction paths — not spotlighted. Trust has to come before utility, always.
What I'd do next
Prototype the physical Pause Kit dispenser UX. Digital and physical must feel like one product — the hardware touchpoint needs the same care and dignity as every screen.
Bigger question
Why does menstrual pain support only exist in apps? The real problem is institutional design — workplaces, campuses, transit. This interface is a starting point, not a solution.
What this taught me
Accessibility isn't a checklist. Emotional safety isn't a brand tone. Inclusive design is the structural choice to center the person most likely to be failed — and build outward from there.
